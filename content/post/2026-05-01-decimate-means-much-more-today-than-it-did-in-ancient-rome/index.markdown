SACHA PFEIFFER, HOST:

Here in the United States, when President Trump talks about the Iran war, he's repeatedly used a particular word to describe U.S. military actions.

(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)

PRESIDENT DONALD TRUMP: We've beaten and completely decimated Iran. They are decimated.

PFEIFFER: Decimate - to destroy or devastate - is our Word of the Week, NPR's series on the origins of popular words. And this word's beginnings might surprise you as NPR's Scott Neuman reports.

SCOTT NEUMAN, BYLINE: The key to the word decimate is the Latin root deci. Think decimeter, one-tenth of a meter. So to decimate something originally meant to destroy one-tenth of it. If that sounds oddly specific, it's because the word comes from a ruthless punishment inflicted on Roman military units accused of mutiny or cowardice.

GREGORY ALDRETE: They would have the group that they wanted to punish randomly draw lots, and every tenth soldier was then clubbed to death by nine others.

NEUMAN: That's Roman historian Gregory S. Aldrete of the University of Wisconsin-Green Bay. He says the most well-documented example came in the early 70s B.C., during the slave uprising led by the famed gladiator Spartacus.

(SOUNDBITE OF FILM, "SPARTACUS")

UNIDENTIFIED ACTOR #1: (As character) I'm Spartacus.

UNIDENTIFIED ACTOR #2: (As character) I'm Spartacus.

UNIDENTIFIED ACTOR #3: (As character) I'm Spartacus.

NEUMAN: Yes, that Spartacus. Barry Strauss, a historian and senior fellow at the Hoover Institution at Stanford, explains that decimation was ordered by General Marcus Licinius Crassus against a military unit that had fled the battlefield.

BARRY STRAUSS: The army had been defeated by Spartacus a number of times, so he might have thought that the army really needed to be shocked out of its behavior.

NEUMAN: So when did the word start getting used to mean wiping out most or all of something? Apparently, sometime in the 17th century. The shift didn't go unnoticed. It quickly became every pedant's pet peeve. For example, take this scene from the TV show "Monk."

(SOUNDBITE OF TV SHOW, "MONK")

MOLLY HAGAN: (As Ms. Lennington) Page 32, the building was decimated.

TONY SHALHOUB: (As Adrian Monk) Technically inaccurate. Decimate means to reduce by one-tenth.

NEUMAN: NPR copy editor Preeti Aroon has occasionally landed smack in the middle of this still-ongoing semantic struggle. At a previous job, she says, an executive editor approached her about a reader's complaint that decimate used in the story in the modern sense was incorrect.

PREETI AROON, BYLINE: I told the executive editor that sometimes we no longer use exclusively the historical definition, and really a more modern definition has come to dominate.

NEUMAN: And as every writer knows, the copy editor always gets the final word. Scott Neuman, NPR News.

(SOUNDBITE OF MUSIC)
